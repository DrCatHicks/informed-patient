# Symptom Clustering and Diagnostic Classification Criteria

## Purpose and boundary

Patients have a right to know what evidence clinicians actually use to
evaluate their symptoms — not just their own account of feeling unwell, but
the structured criteria a specialist would reach for once a similar symptom
picture is described. This guidance exists to inform, not diagnose. Naming a
classification framework is not the same as applying it: never tell a
patient a framework applies to them, matches their symptoms, or explains
their situation. Present it as what clinicians in that territory use to
evaluate patients presenting with a similar cluster, and let the patient
bring it to their own clinician.

## When to consult this guidance

Consult this file once the branching and co-occurrence questions from Phase 1
have surfaced a real symptom picture, not from the first symptom mentioned.
Invoking a classification framework before enough is known risks the
anchoring risk already named in the Red Flags framework (flag 3): narrowing
the rest of the interview around a framework chosen too early.

Within that window, consult this guidance when:

- The patient wonders aloud whether they might have a particular condition,
  or how a given condition is understood by practitioners, in any phrasing —
  "do I have X," "could this be Y," "I've been reading about Z," "what do
  doctors look for to diagnose Q." Answer this when it is asked.
- A diagnosis has already been suggested by a prior clinician (the existing
  diagnosis-status branching question is the natural point to notice this).
- The co-occurrence answers form a recognizable cluster on their own, even if
  the patient hasn't named a condition. Surfacing this proactively is part of
  the point — the goal is to catch things a patient wouldn't think to
  connect. **Hold it until the Phase 1 to Phase 2 transition** and include
  it in the search framing; do not announce a framework mid-interview.
- The patient asks for a broad, multi-system review, or the picture they've
  described already spans several unrelated body systems — see Comprehensive
  multi-system review below.

Set this aside, or don't raise it at all, when:

- A symptom is mentioned without any wondering attached to it — most of the
  interview should stay in ordinary symptom collection.
- The patient declines this kind of information, the same way a decline is
  respected elsewhere in the interview.
- A diagnosis is already confirmed and the question is about whether it is
  right. See Confirmed diagnosis below for the one exception.

### Confirmed diagnosis

A confirmed diagnosis calls for different information — prognosis, risk,
progression — not diagnostic classification criteria. If the patient asks how
their confirmed condition is diagnosed, or whether they "fit" the criteria,
do not retrieve or run the criteria. Say the confirmed findings already
answer that, that a clinician judges fit, and offer severity, subtype, or
prognosis instead. The exception is a
framework that grades severity or subtype of a condition the patient already
has (NYHA for heart failure is the clear case). Those are fair to explain
when the patient asks: what the levels mean and how clinicians assign them.
Do not assign or re-assign the patient's own level, and do not map their
described symptoms onto a level — that judgment belongs to their clinician.

## How to present this to the patient

Never state that a framework applies, matches, or explains the patient's
symptoms. Present it as information about what evidence-based classification
looks like for the relevant territory, and let the patient decide what to do
with it.

Use a pattern close to: "Clinicians evaluating [symptom cluster] often use
[framework name] to help classify what's going on. I can't tell whether it
applies to you, but you might ask your doctor about it, or read more before
your appointment." Avoid sentences like "this sounds like X" or "you might
have Y" — anything that assigns a diagnosis rather than describing a
framework that exists.

A framework named here does not become a hypothesis by being named. The
competing-hypotheses work in Phase 3 still applies in full. Name one to three
frameworks, chosen as described under Recognized classification frameworks.

The criteria themselves come from the source, not from memory. If the
patient asks what the criteria say, say you will pull them from the source
during the search, then summarize only what was retrieved.

## Recognized classification frameworks

First, check quickly whether the presenting cluster maps to one of the ten
domain names below — this is a fast match by name. If nothing obviously fits,
skip straight to searching for the framework actually used in that clinical
territory rather than staying silent — the same posture as the Phase 2
literature search, which doesn't wait for a fixed list either.

**Choose one to three entries, most relevant first.** Rank each matching
domain by how much of what the patient emphasized it covers: the symptoms
behind their functional impact and the ones they described most. Stop at
three, and name fewer if fewer fit. A weak match (one incidental symptom) is
not a match. Use entry 10 only when no condition-specific entry fits. Read
only the entries you chose.

**Domains:** 1 Headache and neurological · 2 Gastrointestinal · 3
Rheumatologic and autoimmune · 4 Fibromyalgia and chronic widespread pain ·
5 Cardiac · 6 Sleep disorders · 7 Fatigue / ME-CFS · 8 Autonomic dysfunction ·
9 Dizziness and vestibular · 10 Chronic pain, general

### How to use an entry

The entries are search pointers, not citations. Each gives the framework, the
source to look for (authors, title, journal, year), and how accessible it is.
Search for that source and cite only the URL the search returned. Do not copy
an identifier (PMID, DOI, PMCID) from memory into the artifact; identifiers
are deliberately left out of this file so they can't be lifted from it.

If the source is inaccessible — paywalled, moved, or the entry says none
exists — search for an official freely accessible version before falling
back to a summary.

Before presenting a framework, do one quick check for whether it has since
been revised, superseded, or is actively disputed. This is a lightweight
check, not a new literature review — one search, not an investigation. This
table is static and not automatically kept current; a framework listed here
as settled can be revised without this file ever being updated, so treat the
entry as a starting point to verify, not a permanent answer. Entries were last
checked September 2026.

### 1. Headache and neurological
**Framework:** ICHD-3 (International Classification of Headache Disorders,
3rd edition)
**What it classifies:** Migraine, tension-type headache, cluster headache,
and their subtypes.
**Source:** Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache
Society. *The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd
edition.* Cephalalgia. 2018;38(1):1-211. Freely browsable at ichd-3.org.
ICHD-4 is in development and has not been released; ICHD-3 remains the
current published edition.

### 2. Gastrointestinal / gut-brain interaction
**Framework:** Rome V (published 2026, replacing Rome IV)
**What it classifies:** Irritable bowel syndrome, functional dyspepsia, and
other disorders of gut-brain interaction (the term Rome V uses in place of
"functional GI disorders").
**Source:** Start with the Rome V overview in *Gastroenterology* (2026),
"Disorders of Gut-Brain Interaction and the Rome V Process," and the Rome
Foundation's Rome V page. The Rome Foundation's full criteria are a separate,
gated resource; prefer an open journal article. If Rome V criteria can't be
retrieved, Drossman DA, *Functional gastrointestinal disorders: history,
pathophysiology, clinical features, and Rome IV*, Gastroenterology.
2016;150(6):1262-1279, is the prior edition. Say it is the prior edition if
you use it. Rome IV terminology and criteria are still what many clinicians
and existing literature use.

### 3. Rheumatologic and autoimmune
**Framework:** ACR/EULAR classification criteria
**What it classifies:** Condition-specific criteria sets — rheumatoid
arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren's syndrome, and others —
not one shared framework across the domain.
**Source (rheumatoid arthritis, the most commonly relevant):** Aletaha D,
et al. *2010 Rheumatoid arthritis classification criteria: an American
College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism collaborative
initiative.* Arthritis Rheum. 2010;62(9):2569-2581 (published in parallel in
Ann Rheum Dis 2010;69:1580-1588). Search for the matching criteria paper when a different
condition in this domain applies. These are classification criteria for
research and clinical grouping, not stand-alone diagnostic tests; a clinician
may diagnose without a patient meeting them.

### 4. Fibromyalgia and chronic widespread pain
**Framework:** Three sets of criteria are in current use: the ACR 2010/2011
criteria, the ACR 2016 revision, and the AAPT 2019 criteria.
**What it classifies:** Widespread pain alongside a symptom severity scale
(ACR); multisite pain with sleep problems or fatigue lasting three months
(AAPT).
**Source:** Wolfe F, et al. *2016 Revisions to the 2010/2011 fibromyalgia
diagnostic criteria.* Semin Arthritis Rheum. 2016;46(3):319-329. Arnold LM,
et al. *AAPT diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia.* J Pain.
2019;20(6):611-628. This is a live example of parallel frameworks — see
When criteria are disputed below.

### 5. Cardiac
**Framework:** NYHA Functional Classification
**What it classifies:** Heart failure symptom severity by functional
limitation. It grades a condition the patient already has, so it is one of
the severity frameworks covered under Confirmed diagnosis above.
**Source:** Criteria Committee, New York Heart Association. *Nomenclature
and Criteria for Diagnosis of Diseases of the Heart and Great Vessels*, 9th
ed. Boston: Little, Brown & Co; 1994. This is a textbook classification with
no single retrievable journal article; summarize from a reputable clinical
reference you actually retrieve rather than attempting to fetch a paper. The
American Heart Association's professional site has a freely accessible
"Classification of Functional Capacity and Objective Assessment" page. The
class is assigned by the clinician, largely from the patient's own report.

### 6. Sleep disorders
**Framework:** ICSD-3-TR (International Classification of Sleep Disorders,
3rd edition, text revision, 2023; the earlier ICSD-3 dates from 2014)
**What it classifies:** Insomnia, sleep-related breathing disorders such as
sleep apnea, and other sleep disorder subtypes.
**Source:** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine sells the manual as a
print book or e-book; it has no free full text. Do not attempt to fetch or
paraphrase the full criteria. Say plainly that the complete classification
isn't freely accessible, and that a clinician or the AASM's own materials
are the source for it. Do not substitute DSM-5 criteria; psychiatric
classification is out of scope (see below).

### 7. Fatigue / ME-CFS
**Framework:** IOM (2015) diagnostic criteria, with other definitions in
parallel use (Canadian Consensus Criteria; NICE NG206, 2021)
**What it classifies:** Myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue
syndrome.
**Source:** Institute of Medicine. *Beyond Myalgic
Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome: Redefining an Illness.*
Washington, DC: National Academies Press; 2015. Freely readable and
downloadable at nationalacademies.org/read/19012. The NICE guideline (NG206)
is also freely available at nice.org.uk. The sets differ, for example in how
long symptoms must last before diagnosis (IOM six months of fatigue; NICE
allows diagnosis at three months) — see When criteria are disputed below.

### 8. Autonomic dysfunction
**Framework:** Heart Rhythm Society consensus criteria
**What it classifies:** Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).
**Source:** Sheldon RS, et al. *2015 Heart Rhythm Society expert consensus
statement on the diagnosis and treatment of postural tachycardia syndrome,
inappropriate sinus tachycardia, and vasovagal syncope.* Heart Rhythm.
2015;12(6):e41-e63. An open-access copy is available in PubMed Central.

### 9. Dizziness and vestibular symptoms
**Framework:** Bárány Society consensus criteria
**What it classifies:** Vestibular migraine, Ménière's disease, persistent
postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD).
**Source (vestibular migraine, the most commonly relevant):** Lempert T, et
al. *Vestibular migraine: diagnostic criteria.* J Vestib Res.
2012;22(4):167-172, and its 2022 update in the same journal (2022;32(1):1-6),
which added a literature update and left the 2012 criteria unchanged. Use the
update where retrievable. For Ménière's disease or PPPD, search the Bárány Society's
consensus documents page (thebaranysociety.org, "ICVD consensus documents").

### 10. Chronic pain, general
**Framework:** AAPT (ACTTION-APS Pain Taxonomy)
**What it classifies:** A cross-cutting classification approach for chronic
pain conditions generally, when no condition-specific framework above fits.
**Source:** Fillingim RB, et al. *The ACTTION-American Pain Society Pain
Taxonomy (AAPT): an evidence-based and multidimensional approach to
classifying chronic pain conditions.* J Pain. 2014;15(3):241-249. An
open-access copy is available in PubMed Central.

Psychiatric classification (DSM-5) is deliberately not included here. This
skill does not assess mental health; if psychological symptoms come up, note
the connection and move on, per the skill's existing scope boundary.

## When criteria are disputed

Some classification frameworks are actively contested, superseded by a
newer version that hasn't fully replaced the old one in practice, or applied
differently across specialties or countries. Two entries above are concrete
examples. Fibromyalgia has three sets in current use, none a clean
replacement for the others. ME/CFS has the IOM criteria alongside the
Canadian Consensus Criteria and NICE NG206, which differ on duration and on
what must be present.

Say so explicitly rather than silently picking one framework and presenting
it as settled. When two frameworks are both genuinely still in clinical use,
name both and note that clinicians may reasonably use either. Route this
through the Metascience concerns red flag (flag 10 in `references/red-flags.md`)
rather than treating it as a separate mechanism — a contested classification
framework is a contested evidence base, the same category that flag already
covers. Do not resolve the dispute yourself or imply one framework is more
correct; that judgment belongs to the patient's clinician.

## When no consensus criteria exists

Some presentations genuinely have no agreed symptom-based diagnostic
criteria — endometriosis, diagnosed primarily through imaging and surgery
rather than a symptom checklist, is a clear example. When this is the case,
say so explicitly rather than omitting the topic or implying a framework
exists where none does: "There isn't a single agreed symptom-based framework
for this, and diagnosis usually depends on [clinical judgment/imaging/testing/etc.]."

Do not assemble a symptom checklist from memory to stand in for criteria. The
patient's own symptom inventory (frequency, severity, duration, functional
impact) is what to bring to the appointment.

## Comprehensive multi-system review

Do not run a full review-of-systems sweep by default. Offer it only when:

- The patient explicitly asks for a broad or comprehensive look across their
  health, not just the presenting issue, or
- The scope they've already described spans clearly unrelated body systems,
  suggesting the presenting complaint may not be the whole picture.

When offered and accepted, work through only these categories, which are the
standard ones a clinician would screen: constitutional (fever, weight change,
fatigue, night sweats), HEENT, cardiovascular, respiratory,
gastrointestinal, genitourinary, musculoskeletal, neurological, skin,
endocrine, hematologic/lymphatic, and allergic/immunologic. Skip categories
that plainly don't apply. For each category, ask one open question ("Any
changes with your skin? Anything new or different?"), with no example
symptoms added, and follow up only on what the patient reports. Mood and psychological symptoms are not a category
here; if the patient raises them, note them for their medical team and move
on, per the scope boundary. This is a wider net, not a diagnostic tool —
treat anything it surfaces the same way as anything else in Phase 1:
gathered, not interpreted. Record what it surfaces in the artifact under
Additional Systems Reviewed.
