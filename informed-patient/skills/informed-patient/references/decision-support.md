# Decision Support: Structure the Choice, Never Make It

Use this when the user faces a treatment decision (whether to have a procedure, which one, or when) and asks for help thinking it through. It draws on the shared decision-making literature, including the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) and the Ottawa Decision Support Framework. The aim is an informed decision that reflects the patient's own values, not any particular choice.

The skill's guardrails apply unchanged: do not recommend a treatment, do not rank the options, and do not replace the clinician's judgment.

## 1. Name the decision

Write the decision in one sentence, in the patient's terms. Example: "Whether to have knee replacement surgery now, wait and re-check in six months, or keep managing with physical therapy." Then note who decides (the patient), who advises (which clinicians), and any deadline.

## 2. Option grid

Columns are options. Always include the "no procedure now" option, written as it is actually practised ("supervised watchful management with scheduled re-checks"), not as "do nothing". Rows are the same for every option:

| Row | What to put |
|---|---|
| What it involves | One plain sentence: what happens, hospital stay, recovery aids |
| Chance of benefit | Absolute frequency at a stated time point, e.g. "about 70 in 100 improve by 1 year" |
| Common downsides | Most frequent side effects, as absolute frequencies |
| Serious risks | Rare but grave events, as absolute frequencies |
| Chance of needing more treatment | Within a stated time window |
| Recovery burden | Time off work or driving, restrictions, help needed at home |
| Who it suits / rules it out | Health factors that guidelines name |
| Strength of evidence | RCT, cohort, or expert opinion; note where clinicians disagree |

Rules:

- **Absolute, not relative.** Convert "40% higher odds" into "about 7 in 100 instead of about 5 in 100". When only relative effects are available, give the baseline and say the conversion is approximate.
- **Name the population** behind each number (age, sex, severity, setting). Mark ⚠️ when it differs from the patient.
- **Same time frame across a row**, or flag it when it differs.
- **Show disagreement.** If good sources differ, give the range and both sources rather than averaging them.
- **Every option needs evidence.** Search until each option on the grid has at least one relevant source, even if that runs past the usual 5-10 sources. If an option or row has none, say so in the cell; that gap is a finding.
- **No ranking.** No ordering by preference, no colours or symbols that imply a winner, no "best option".

## 3. Personal risk

Population rates are a starting point. List what would personalise them, as items to do with the clinician:

- **Validated risk calculators** where they exist (for example, the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator for many operations). These are best run with the clinician, using the patient's actual clinical details.
- **Factors in the patient's history** that the literature links to higher or lower risk. State each as a question for the clinician, not as a verdict.

## 4. Values clarification (for the patient)

Write 4-6 questions that make the trade-offs explicit, using the patient's own stated goals from the interview. Examples:

- "How much does getting back to [activity they named] in the next few months matter, compared with avoiding a small risk of a serious complication?"
- "How do you feel about a small chance of a serious problem now, set against a larger chance of slow change later?"
- "Who would be with you in the first week, and does that change which timing works?"

These are for the patient to answer. Record answers in their own words when they arrive; never infer them. If the user is helping someone else, list these under **Ask Them First**.

## 5. Readiness check (SURE)

SURE is a four-item yes/no screen for decisional conflict, validated for use in clinical settings (Légaré et al., 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20705870/; Ferron Parayre et al., 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23776141/). Offer it to the patient:

- **S**ure of myself: Do you feel sure about the best choice for you?
- **U**nderstand information: Do you know the benefits and risks of each option?
- **R**isk–benefit ratio: Are you clear about which benefits and risks matter most to you?
- **E**ncouragement: Do you have enough support and advice to make a choice?

Any "no" points to a next step, framed as something to bring to the appointment: more information, more time with the values questions, or more support.

## 6. What not to do

- Do not answer "should I have it?" or "should they have it?" Answer with the grid and the questions, and say once why: "That decision is yours to make with your medical team. Here's a way to lay the options side by side."
- Do not frame watchful management as giving up, or a procedure as the brave choice.
- Do not present one clinician's preference as the standard when the evidence is mixed. Present it as a question.
